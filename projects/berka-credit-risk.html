What predicts a bad loan at a retail bank

Every loan on an account that had been overdrawn in the prior year went bad. Two screens the bank can run before approval bring the problematic loan rate from 11.1% to 5.0%.

Program
RevoU capstone project (DEEPP, Milestones 0 to 3)
Date
September 2026
Tools
Python, pandas, DuckDB (SQL), Tableau
Deliverables
21-slide deck, notebook, dashboard

The question

How can the credit team bring the problematic loan rate from 11% to below 5% within 12 months, using signals the bank already has before a loan is paid out?

Context

The Berka dataset is a public Czech retail banking dataset covering 1993 to 1998: 5,369 clients, 4,500 accounts, about 1.05 million transactions and 682 loans. 76 of those loans ended in default or arrears, which puts the problematic loan rate at 11.1%.

What I did

  1. Framed the problem with DARCI, a one-sentence SMART problem statement and an issue tree with four root causes: weak account liquidity, cash flow strain, loan burden and thin banking history.
  2. Joined eight tables in DuckDB into one row per loan, using only transactions dated before the loan was granted so the analysis reflects what an underwriter could actually see.
  3. Tested each root cause in Python, ranked the drivers by risk lift and checked them with a simple logistic regression.
  4. Built a risk score, simulated the effect of each screening rule on the portfolio and published a Tableau dashboard for the credit team.

What I found

  • All 27 loans on overdrawn accounts failed. They account for 35.5% of every bad loan in the portfolio, so the strongest warning signal already sits in the bank's own data.
  • Affordability matters, not loan size. Loans in the lowest quartile of payment to monthly inflow failed 5.8% of the time, against 19.3% in the highest quartile.
  • Behaviour beats demographics. The riskiest 20% of the behaviour-based score hold 48 of the 76 bad loans, while customer demographics barely separate good loans from bad ones.
  • The 1998 improvement is not real yet. Its 2.5% rate reflects loans that have not had time to fail yet, against 13 to 14% for mature cohorts.

What I recommended

  • Send any applicant with an overdraft in the last 12 months to manual review. Alone this takes the rate from 11.1% to 7.5% without losing a good loan.
  • Add minimum balance, average balance and the overdraft flag to the scorecard and review the top 20% of scores. Combined with the first rule this lands at 5.0%.
  • Keep a payment to inflow cap of 0.17 as a buffer, since 5.0% sits exactly on the target.
  • Report the KPI by loan vintage at equal loan age so the team does not read maturity bias as progress.
